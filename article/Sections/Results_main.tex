\subsection{\blt{Results on the 2023--2025 Test Period}} \label{s:results}
Table~\ref{tab:bench_mean} reports the means over the 200 portfolios, Table~\ref{tab:bench_paired} the paired tests and Fig.~\ref{fig:bench_wealth} the median wealth.

With the corrected optimiser the Risk-Averse policy (RA) reaches an annualised Sharpe ratio of $0.34$ and a total return of $1.11$, against $0.66$ and $1.27$ for the daily rebalanced equal-weight portfolio ($p<0.001$, RA better in $26\%$ of the portfolios). The cause is in the last two columns: RA trades $15\%$ of the portfolio per day and pays $15\%$ of the initial wealth in costs over the two years, ten times the equal-weight portfolio, while before costs the two are comparable. On risk it does what its name promises: its maximum drawdown $-25.5\%$ is smaller than that of both equal-weight portfolios and far smaller than that of every mean-variance variant ($-40\%$ to $-42\%$), and only the 250-day minimum-variance portfolio is better.

The turnover has a single cause. The risk weight $\omega^{\ast}$ of Sec.~\ref{s:FPD} is returned as the upper end $\omega_{\max}=10^3$ of the searched interval in all $10^5$ decision steps, because the necessary condition for an extreme is a sum of terms that are positive for $\mat{D}\succ\mat{O}$, $\hat{\mat{\Sigma}}\succeq\mat{O}$, $\omega>0$ and therefore has no root. At $\omega=10^3$ the variance term of \eqref{e:lossmatrixQdefinition} exceeds the transaction-cost term by four orders of magnitude, so the cost effectively leaves the loss and the allocation is re-optimised from scratch every day. Sec.~\ref{s:sensitivity} treats $\omega_{\max}$ as the user-set quantity it is; this is also the mechanism behind the small drawdowns.

The legacy softmax policy, which produced the tables of the previous version, keeps the weights within a daily turnover of $2.9\%$ of uniform. Under a common cost model it is indistinguishable from the rebalanced equal-weight portfolio in return and $0.03$ below it in Sharpe ratio ($p<0.001$). Our earlier claim of outperforming the uniform portfolio rested on charging that portfolio no costs and does not survive a paired test. Among the classical benchmarks, 250-day mean-variance gives the highest return ($1.57$) at a $-40\%$ drawdown, while the ARX-conditional mean-variance loses money ($0.73$): the same regressors are not tradeable through a classical estimator and optimiser. The S\&P~500 returned $1.62$ at a Sharpe ratio of $1.58$ over these days, which no strategy on this 20-stock universe approaches, equal-weight buy-and-hold included; we therefore make no claim against the index.
