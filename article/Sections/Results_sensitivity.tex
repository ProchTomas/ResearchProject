\subsection{\blt{What the Components Contribute}} \label{s:ablation_results}\label{s:sensitivity}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} ablates the components and Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} varies the user-set quantities, both on 100 portfolios.

\emph{Essential.} Removing the FPD risk term is catastrophic: the policy follows the point forecast, turns the portfolio over $1.5$ times per day and loses $72\%$. Thompson sampling of $\theta$ ($-56\%$) and dropping the structure estimation ($-49\%$) do the same, for the same reason. \emph{Inert.} The corrected SMALBE and the SLSQP reference give identical allocations, the largest weight difference being $3\cdot10^{-7}$, and $h=5$ reproduces $h=1$ to $7\cdot10^{-6}$ because at $\omega=10^3$ the one-step loss dominates the loss-to-go. Replacing the data-based forgetting by a plain Bayesian update changes nothing measurable (Sharpe $+0.04$, $p=0.13$) and the forgetting prior $(\alpha_0,\beta_0)$ moves the weights but no metric. Fig.~\ref{fig:bench_forgetting} explains this: the optimiser holds $\gamma$, the weight of the prior, between $0.5$ and $0.8$ on almost every day, so the statistic is pulled back towards $\mat{V}_0$ faster than data accumulate. The flat prior looks like the best variant (Sharpe $0.72$) for a trivial reason: with $\mat{V}_0=10^{-6}\mat{I}$ the forecasts vanish, the covariance is isotropic and the constrained minimiser is exactly $1/N$. Of the tuned quantities only $\mu$ matters, and $0.9$ is not its best value: $\mu=0.7$ keeps 15 of 137 regressors instead of 57 and gains $0.18$ in Sharpe ratio ($p<0.001$), still below the equal-weight portfolio.

\emph{The risk weight.} Capping the searched interval isolates the mechanism of Sec.~\ref{s:results}. At $\omega_{\max}=1$ the search returns $\omega\approx10^{-5}$, the return term leaves the loss, $a_t=a_{t-1}$ becomes optimal and the policy reproduces the rebalanced equal-weight portfolio to three decimals. At $\omega_{\max}=10$ or $100$ the forecasts dominate the variance term, the policy takes corner positions in single stocks and loses $73\%$ and $46\%$. Three principled elicitations used in place of the bound, namely a unit Kullback--Leibler divergence between model and ideal, a mean shift matching the predicted return, and a fixed $\omega=40$, all lying between $27$ and $91$ in $80\%$ of the steps, lose $63$--$65\%$. Only $\omega=10^3$, where the forecasts are effectively ignored, leaves a usable policy. The forecasts explain this: over the test period they have the same standard deviation as the realised returns ($0.027$), a correlation of $-0.01$ with them, a sign accuracy of $49.7\%$ and twice the squared error of predicting zero. A loss that takes them at face value trades on noise, so no analytical form of $\omega^{\ast}$ can repair the design; the point forecast has to be shrunk, or the posterior uncertainty integrated into the design, before it enters the loss.

\subsection{\blt{Stress Tests}} \label{s:stress}
Fig.~\ref{fig:stress_synthetic} uses ARX data with known parameters (4 assets, 6 regressors, 700 days), two one-day crashes of $-12\%$ on all assets, and a sign flip of all coefficients at day 550. On the crash days the data-based forgetting sets $\beta=0$ and $\gamma\approx1$: the record is discarded, the statistic reverts to the prior and the estimation error does not move, whereas both other learners absorb the outlier. This is the robustness claimed in Sec.~\ref{s:forgetting}, and it holds. The same figure shows its price. During the stationary phase the error stays at its prior level ($0.72$) while the others improve to $0.5$, and after the parameter change the weights return at once to their prior values and the model does not re-learn, while exponential forgetting halves the error within 150 days. The mechanism protects against outliers by not learning, which is also why removing it costs nothing in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.
